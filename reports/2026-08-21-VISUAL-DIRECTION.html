Visual direction — the mycontext web-UI mockup

A panel commissioned 2026-08-21. Everything below was measured today, in a browser, against docs/design/web-ui-mockup.html as it stands on v2/review-t1 — not inherited from the panel that ran on 19 August. Where a prior finding no longer reproduces, that is said plainly.

How this was measured. The mockup was served over HTTP (127.0.0.1, a throwaway node:http server) and driven with Playwright 1.62 / Chromium at 1280×900, deviceScaleFactor 1, timezone UTC. All 21 screens were visited in English and Hebrew and in light and dark — 84 states. Contrast was computed from getComputedStyle on every element that owns visible text, compositing the whole ancestor background chain and the .gloss sheen gradient at that element's own vertical position; foreground alpha is composited over the resulting background. Colours resolve through a live probe element, so light-dark() and color-mix(in oklch, …) are read as the browser paints them, never parsed out of the stylesheet. Depth and ribbon colours were additionally sampled from real rendered pixels out of PNG screenshots. Zero console errors, zero uncaught exceptions and zero dialogs across all 84 states.

Provenance. M measured today   V verified by reading the file   R reasoned. Nothing is marked M that was not re-measured today.

1. Defects — not a matter of taste

Ranked by severity. These are not weighed against preferences; they are things the page does that it should not do.

D1 Printing while the dark theme is on produces an unreadable page

Severity criticalScreens all 21 Languages bothFix one CSS line

M Under @media print with data-theme="dark", the 21 screens produce 246 text-contrast failures. The same pass in light produces 17. The print block forces body{background:#fff;color:#000} but never resets color-scheme, so every light-dark() token keeps its dark value while the paper turns white.

Why nothing caught it. V e2e/print.spec.ts does assert bodyBg === rgb(255,255,255) and bodyColor === rgb(0,0,0) — and both still pass, because the body is the one thing the print block does reset. D7 Under Windows High Contrast the tier ribbon and the relations graph lose their meaning

M With forced-colors: active, .gloss degrades exactly as designed. But .seg.pinned, .seg.index and .dot all resolve to background rgb(255,255,255) / border rgb(0,0,0) in light and the inverse in dark — four tiers, one visual state. And SVG is not adjusted at all: the relations graph keeps stroke: rgb(147,48,47) and node fill: rgb(23,23,28) against a system Canvas, so in dark High Contrast the nodes sit at roughly 1.18:1 against the ground.

The chips survive, because their ::before glyph is content and content is not forced. The ribbon has no glyph and the graph has no adjustment. Both are fixable inside the existing @media (forced-colors:active) block — the segments need a pattern (repeating-linear-gradient survives; it is a background-image), the SVG needs stroke:CanvasText / fill:Canvas written by name.

2. Palette — and can it go blue?

Yes, and the interesting part is which half goes blue. The palette has two independent halves, and treating them as one is what makes the question feel risky:

  • The neutral axis — --paper, --panel, --sink, --rule, --edge, --ink, --dim, --faint. This is what people mean when they say a product "looks blue". It carries no meaning; it is temperature.
  • The accent — --gold. This one carries meaning: this governs, unconditionally. Spending it on brand is spending the only place the colour says something.

R There is also a third slot that does not exist yet and should: a colour that means "this is clickable". Its absence is measurable — it is why .linkid (D3) is unstyled and why the focus ring had to borrow gold.

4. Depth, and how far the floating cards should go

M The depth system already exists: --rim-lit, --rim-shade, --sheen-top, --sheen-bottom, plus .gloss on 43 elements and .gloss.float for a raised tier. It is implemented the right way — an opaque background colour with a painted gradient and two zero-blur inset shadows, no backdrop-filter, and it degrades under all three preference queries and under print.

And in light mode, half of it is a no-op. M Sampled from real rendered pixels: the top edge of a light card measures rgb(255,255,255) and its interior rgb(255,255,254) — a ratio of 1.00:1. The white rim highlight has nothing to be lighter than, because --panel is already #fffffe. Only the bottom shade registers, at 1.13:1. In dark the same sample gives 1.25:1 at the top and 1.06:1 at the bottom — the rim is doing real work there.

5. Is this the most attractive design available?

R Honestly: it is unusually coherent for a hand-written sheet, and the gap between how it looks and how a finished product looks is not the palette. Three measured things account for most of it, and all three are already itemised above:

  1. An unstyled control repeated five times per screen (D3). Native buttons in a table are the single loudest "this is a mockup" signal on any screen.
  2. Light mode has no depth at all (§4) — the rim highlight measures 1.00:1. Dark mode looks materially better than light for this one reason.
  3. Fourteen font sizes where six are declared (§3), including one heading larger than the screen title.

None of those is a restyle. R A palette swap would change the photograph; these three change whether it reads as drawn or as built.

7. The one question

Does --gold keep its monopoly on meaning — so that blue enters only as chrome, for links and focus, and never marks a tier — or is gold available to be spent as the brand colour, in which case the product needs a different mark for "this governs" before anything is written into the mockup?

Every ranked option above turns on this and only this. Options 1, 2 and 3 assume the first answer. Option 4 assumes the second and has to name the replacement mark. The type and depth options are independent of it and can be ruled on separately.
